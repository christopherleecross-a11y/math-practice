@@ calc-3-1 | Example 1
dy/dx = dy/du · du/dx || Example 1. The Chain Rule: d y d x equals d y d u times d u d x.
dy/du = 5u⁴,  du/dx = 2x || The derivative of u to the fifth is 5 u to the fourth. The derivative of x squared plus 1 is 2 x.
dy/dx = 5(x² + 1)⁴ · 2x = 10x(x² + 1)⁴ || Put u back in and multiply: 10 x times x squared plus 1 to the fourth.

@@ calc-3-1 | Example 2
h′(x) = f′(g(x)) · g′(x) || Example 2. Chain Rule: the outside's derivative, evaluated at the inside, times the inside's derivative.
h′(1) = f′(g(1)) · g′(1) || Put in x equals 1.
g(1) = 2 → f′(2) · g′(1) = 3(−4) || g of 1 is 2, so we need f prime of 2, which is 3. g prime of 1 is negative 4.
h′(1) = −12 || 3 times negative 4 is negative 12. Notice we never needed f prime of 5.

@@ calc-3-1 | Example 3
outside: u⁴ → 4u³;  inside: 3x² − 5 → 6x || Example 3. The outside is something to the fourth. The inside is 3 x squared minus 5, with derivative 6 x.
f′(x) = 4(3x² − 5)³ · 6x = 24x(3x² − 5)³ || 4 times 6 x is 24 x.
f′(1) = 24(1)(3 − 5)³ = 24(−8) = −192 || At 1: 3 minus 5 is negative 2, cubed is negative 8. Times 24: negative 192.

@@ calc-3-1 | Example 4
a.  (eᵘ)′ = eᵘ · u′ || Example 4, part a. The derivative of e to the u is e to the u times u prime.
a.  u = x² − 3x → u′ = 2x − 3 → (2x − 3)e^(x² − 3x) || The exponent's derivative is 2 x minus 3.
b.  (sin u)′ = cos u · u′ → 4 cos(4x) || Part b. The derivative of sine u is cosine u times u prime. The inside 4 x has derivative 4.

@@ calc-3-1 | Example 5
y(2) = (4 − 3)³ = 1 || Example 5. The point: at x equals 2, y is 1 cubed, 1.
y′ = 3(x² − 3)² · 2x || Chain Rule: 3 times the inside squared, times 2 x.
y′(2) = 3(1)²(4) = 12 || At 2: 3 times 1 times 4 is 12.
y − 1 = 12(x − 2) → y = 12x − 23 || The tangent line: y equals 12 x minus 23.

@@ calc-3-2 | Example 1
2x + 2y · dy/dx = 0 || Example 1. Differentiate both sides with respect to x. y is a function of x, so the derivative of y squared is 2 y times d y d x.
dy/dx = −2x/(2y) = −x/y || Solve for d y d x: negative x over y.
at (3, −4): −3/(−4) = 3/4 || At 3, negative 4: negative 3 over negative 4 is 3 fourths.

@@ calc-3-2 | Example 2
2x + (y + x · dy/dx) + 2y · dy/dx = 0 || Example 2. Differentiate each term. x y needs the Product Rule: y plus x d y d x.
dy/dx(x + 2y) = −(2x + y) || Collect the d y d x terms and factor.
dy/dx = −(2x + y)/(x + 2y) || Divide.
at (1, 2): −(2 + 2)/(1 + 4) = −4/5 || At 1, 2: negative 4 fifths.

@@ calc-3-2 | Example 3
dy/dx = −(2x + 3y)/(3x + 2y) || Example 3. Differentiate the same way as Example 2. The 3 x y term gives 3 y plus 3 x d y d x.
at (1, −2): −(2 − 6)/(3 − 4) = −(−4)/(−1) = −4 || At 1, negative 2: the top is negative 4 and the bottom is negative 1. The slope is negative 4.
y + 2 = −4(x − 1) → y = −4x + 2 || The tangent line is y equals negative 4 x plus 2.

@@ calc-3-2 | Example 4
y′ = −x/y || Example 4. From Example 1, y prime is negative x over y.
y″ = −(y · 1 − x · y′)/y² || Differentiate again with the Quotient Rule.
= −(y + x²/y)/y² = −(y² + x²)/y³ || Replace y prime with negative x over y, then multiply the top and bottom by y.
= −25/y³ || x squared plus y squared is 25.

@@ calc-3-2 | Example 5
a.  (2/3)x^(2/3 − 1) = (2/3)x^(−1/3) || Example 5, part a. The Power Rule works for fractional exponents too. 2 thirds minus 1 is negative one third.
b.  y = (x² + 1)^(1/3) || Part b. Write the cube root as the one third power.
b.  y′ = (1/3)(x² + 1)^(−2/3) · 2x || Chain Rule: bring down one third, subtract 1, and multiply by 2 x.
b.  = 2x/(3(x² + 1)^(2/3)) || Move the negative exponent to the bottom.

@@ calc-3-3 | Example 1
(f⁻¹)′(a) = 1/f′(f⁻¹(a)) || Example 1. The derivative of an inverse is 1 over f prime, evaluated at the matching x.
f(1) = 1 + 1 + 1 = 3 → g(3) = 1 || Which x gives f of x equals 3? Try 1: it works. So g of 3 is 1.
f′(x) = 3x² + 1 → f′(1) = 4 || f prime of 1 is 4.
g′(3) = 1/4 || So g prime of 3 is one fourth.

@@ calc-3-3 | Example 2
f(2) = 7 → g(7) = 2 || Example 2. Since f of 2 is 7, the inverse sends 7 back to 2.
g′(7) = 1/f′(2) = 1/(−5) = −1/5 || The slope of the inverse is the reciprocal: negative one fifth.

@@ calc-3-3 | Example 3
(sin⁻¹x)′ = 1/√(1 − x²) || Example 3. The derivative of inverse sine is 1 over the square root of 1 minus x squared.
f′(1/2) = 1/√(1 − 1/4) = 1/√(3/4) = 2/√3 || At one half: 1 minus one fourth is 3 fourths. Its root is root 3 over 2, and the reciprocal is 2 over root 3.
= 2√3/3 || Rationalized: 2 root 3 over 3.
(cos⁻¹x)′ = −1/√(1 − x²) → −1 at x = 0 || Inverse cosine has the same formula with a negative. At 0, that's negative 1.

@@ calc-3-3 | Example 4
(tan⁻¹x)′ = 1/(1 + x²) || Example 4. The derivative of inverse tangent is 1 over 1 plus x squared.
f′(√3) = 1/(1 + 3) = 1/4 || At root 3: 1 over 4.
f′(−1) = 1/(1 + 1) = 1/2 || At negative 1: 1 over 2.

@@ calc-3-3 | Example 5
a.  u = 3x: u′/(1 + u²) = 3/(1 + 9x²) || Example 5, part a. Chain Rule: u prime over 1 plus u squared. u is 3 x, so u prime is 3 and u squared is 9 x squared.
b.  u = x²: u′/√(1 − u²) = 2x/√(1 − x⁴) || Part b. u is x squared, so u prime is 2 x, and u squared is x to the fourth.

@@ calc-3-4 | Example 1
(ln u)′ = u′/u || Example 1. The derivative of ln u is u prime over u.
a.  2x/(x² + 5) || Part a. The derivative of the inside, 2 x, over the inside.
b.  1 · ln x + x · (1/x) = ln x + 1 || Part b. Product Rule: the derivative of x is 1, and of ln x is 1 over x. x times 1 over x is 1.

@@ calc-3-4 | Example 2
f(x) = ln(2x + 1) − ln(x + 3) || Example 2. Use the log rule first: the log of a quotient is a difference. That makes the derivative much easier.
f′(x) = 2/(2x + 1) − 1/(x + 3) || Differentiate each: u prime over u.
f′(0) = 2/1 − 1/3 = 5/3 || At 0: 2 minus one third is 5 thirds.

@@ calc-3-4 | Example 3
a.  (logₐ x)′ = 1/(x ln a) → 1/(x ln 2) || Example 3, part a. For a log with base a, the derivative is 1 over x ln a.
b.  (aˣ)′ = aˣ ln a → 5ˣ ln 5 || Part b. For a to the x, the derivative is a to the x times ln a.

@@ calc-3-4 | Example 4
ln y = ln xˣ = x ln x || Example 4. The variable is in both the base and the exponent, so take ln of both sides. The exponent comes down.
y′/y = ln x + 1 || Differentiate both sides. The left side is y prime over y. The right side is from Example 1, part b.
y′ = y(ln x + 1) = xˣ(ln x + 1) || Multiply by y, which is x to the x.

@@ calc-3-4 | Example 5
lim(n→∞) (1 + a/n)ⁿ = eᵃ || Example 5. Use the limit that defines e: 1 plus a over n, to the n, approaches e to the a.
a = 2 → e² || Here a is 2, so the limit is e squared.
